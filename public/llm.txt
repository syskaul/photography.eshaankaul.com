# Eshaan Kaul

## About

Eshaan Kaul is a photographer of Kashmiri descent based in the New York metro
area. He photographs architecture, patterns, and the systems that shape daily
life.

Kaul photographs the built and organized world: the architecture, patterns, and
unseen systems that shape how people live, gather, and work. He brings a patient
eye to light and geometry, and a documentary instinct to the question of how
spaces shape the people who move through them.

He is drawn to places where order is visible. Light, line, shadow, and repetition
are the tools he uses to read these structures, finding rhythm in ordered spaces
and meaning in the small gestures that disturb them. Beneath the work runs a
single question: how do we build the worlds we inhabit, and how do they, in turn,
build us? Through his photographs, Kaul seeks a point of contact between the
viewer and the subject, a moment in which a structure becomes a place and a
figure becomes a presence.

## Three ways of looking

One subject, approached through three lenses:

1. **Structure and Light** — Architecture sets the stage, and light is how Kaul
   reads it. Light does not simply illuminate a structure; it discloses it,
   tracing edges and deepening shadows until a figure enters and lends the space
   scale, threshold, and consequence, when geometry softens into something
   lived. Inspired by [Rinko Kawauchi](https://rinkokawauchi.com/).
2. **Order and Interruption** — Patterns, repetition, and the hidden choreography
   of work form a quiet grammar. He photographs the rhythm, then waits for the
   gesture that breaks it. Inspired by
   [Sasha Arutyunova](https://www.sashaarutyunova.com/).
3. **Witness and Archive** — Rooted in a documentary instinct, the photograph
   becomes both record and memory, an attempt to hold the ordinary built world
   before it fades. Inspired by [Rob Stephenson](https://www.robstephenson.com/)
   and [Steve McCurry](https://www.stevemccurry.com/).

## Background and equipment

Kaul's first encounter with a camera came in childhood, when he found his
father's 110-format pocket camera, loaded with the tiny cartridge film Kodak
introduced in 1972. The deeper awakening came in a physics class, drawing rays
of light through convex and concave lenses and watching them gather to a point
or scatter apart. He took from those diagrams a way of seeing: a sliver of glass
can decide what the world becomes, and light is never neutral.

After his school recognized his work, the fascination became a practice. He
began photographing with a Canon 550D in 2010, which he still owns. His recent
work is made mostly with a Fujifilm X100V and, just as often, his iPhone.
